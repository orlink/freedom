What is freedom?

We define freedom, with respect to a specific individual or shared interest, as the ability to self-govern in pursuit of that interest: to have the institutions and the means to make and implement decisions about it without outside interference.

People’s most fundamental interest is the ability to form their own individual and shared interests in the first place: to decide for themselves what matters to them and with whom they share it.

People make and implement decisions in one of three ways: directly, through representatives, or through a selfless entity such as a machine.

The established mechanisms of direct decision-making include the general assembly and the referendum. A proper referendum is legally binding, has no participation quorum and puts concrete proposals to the vote. It comes in three main types: the citizen initiative, the mandatory referendum and the veto referendum.

A representative is a person or entity elected by the people, empowered to act on their behalf with respect to some of their interests, and individually accountable to his or her constituents. The last part is crucial. Someone who makes decisions for us, whom we may have elected but cannot hold accountable, is by definition a tyrant. A public representative should therefore be elected through an electoral system, such as the single transferable vote, that prioritizes voting for individual candidates and not for groups of candidates. Without individual accountability there is no representation and by extension no freedom.

It follows that self-governing institutions should be organized around specific shared interests, so that people or entities who do not hold those interests cannot take part in deciding them, either directly or through representatives.

A large city government, for example, should preferably not be empowered to decide neighborhood-specific matters such as playgrounds or walkways. Otherwise, residents of other neighborhoods, who do not live there and do not normally use its amenities, would be deciding for that neighborhood through their representatives in the city government. This is the curse of overly centralized government, and it typically shows up as neglect or mismanagement of lower-level interests.

By the same token, neighborhoods should be self-governing with respect to their neighborhood specific interests. Each neighborhood should also have at least one representative at the higher level government - the municipal council. That representative’s first job is to protect the neighborhood’s ability to govern itself; only their second is to take part in governing shared municipal interests such as public transportation.
